Chasing an Invisible Angle: EKF vs. UKF for Vehicle Side-Slip Estimation

March 21, 2022
Vehicle Dynamics
B.Sc. Thesis
#KalmanFilter #EKF #UKF #VehicleDynamics #StateEstimation #MATLAB
Abstract: The side-slip angle $\beta$, the angle between where a car points and where it actually travels, is one of the clearest signs of whether a vehicle is still under control. No series-production sensor measures it. My B.Sc. thesis implemented two variants of the Kalman filter, the Extended (EKF) and the Unscented (UKF), to estimate $\beta$ from the sensors an ESC system already carries, and compared them in MATLAB/Simulink. The comparison turned out to be more interesting than "the UKF is more accurate". In everyday driving the two are indistinguishable. At the tyre's grip limit the UKF recovers where the EKF becomes overconfident. And the same sigma points that help it there make it the more fragile filter when its spread is tuned carelessly.

1. The Angle Nobody Measures

Watch a car drive through a corner from above. Its body points one way, but its centre of gravity moves along a slightly different direction. The angle between the two is the side-slip angle:

// Side-slip angle at the centre of gravity
$$\beta = \arctan\frac{v_y}{v_x}$$

In normal driving on a dry road, $\beta$ stays within a couple of degrees and nobody notices it. When it grows, the car is sliding: the tyres are near their limit, steering loses authority over the car's rotation, and a skid is under way. That is why Electronic Stability Control (ESC) cares so much about it. Knowing $\beta$ is knowing how close the car is to losing control.

The trouble is that measuring it directly takes optical speed-over-ground sensors or dual-antenna GNSS/INS units. These are the instruments of a test vehicle, far too expensive for a production car. A production car has to estimate $\beta$ from the sensors it already carries.

What the ESC cluster measures
Cheap, fast, slightly wrong
  • yaw rate $r$ (gyro)
  • lateral acceleration $a_y$ (accelerometer)
  • longitudinal speed $v_x$ (wheel speeds)
  • steering-wheel angle $\delta_{SW}$

Each comes with noise and a small offset that drifts with temperature and age.

What we want
One angle, never measured

$\beta$ appears in none of these signals directly. It has to be inferred through a model of how the car moves, which is exactly the job a Kalman filter was made for.

Why not just integrate?

For constant speed and small angles, pure kinematics already links $\beta$ to two of the measured signals, with no model parameters at all:

// Kinematic relation
$$\dot\beta \approx \frac{a_y}{v_x} - r$$

It is tempting to integrate this and call it a day. The catch is the word integrate: every hundredth of a m/s² of accelerometer offset and every tenth of a degree per second of gyro offset is summed up forever. In the run below the integral has drifted by about 0.75° after twelve seconds of gentle slalom, which is more than the entire signal it is supposed to follow. Nothing in the equation ever pulls it back.

So we need a model that ties $\beta$ to quantities we can measure, and a filter that weighs that model against noisy measurements. That is the Kalman filter's job.

2. A Model of the Car

The estimator's state is $\mathbf{x} = [\beta,\ r]^\top$. Its inputs are the measured steering angle and speed, $\mathbf{u} = [\delta,\ v_x]^\top$. Newton's second law in the lateral direction and around the vertical axis gives the nonlinear single-track model:

// Nonlinear single-track model
$$\begin{aligned} \dot\beta &= \frac{\cos^2\beta}{v_x}\left(\frac{F_{y,f}\cos\delta + F_{y,r}}{m} - v_x\, r\right)\\ \dot r &= \frac{l_f\, F_{y,f}\cos\delta - l_r\, F_{y,r}}{I_z} \end{aligned}$$

The tyre forces depend on how much each axle slips, which in turn depends on the state itself:

// Slip angles
$$\alpha_f = \delta - \arctan\!\left(\tan\beta + \frac{l_f\, r}{v_x}\right), \qquad \alpha_r = -\arctan\!\left(\tan\beta - \frac{l_r\, r}{v_x}\right)$$

The tyre is where the trouble lives. For small slip it behaves like a linear spring. Beyond roughly ten degrees it saturates: more slip no longer buys more force. Pacejka's Magic Formula captures this shape with four coefficients. Friction $\mu$ enters through the similarity method, so that a wet road keeps the same cornering stiffness but runs out of grip sooner:

// Magic Formula tyre (per axle)
$$F_y(\alpha) = \mu F_z \sin\!\Big(C \arctan\big(B\tilde\alpha - E\,(B\tilde\alpha - \arctan B\tilde\alpha)\big)\Big), \qquad \tilde\alpha = \frac{\alpha}{\mu}$$

The measurement model is short, and it is where the nonlinearity bites. The gyro sees $r$ directly, but the accelerometer sees $\beta$ only through the tyre forces:

// Measurement model
$$\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} r \\ a_y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} r \\ \big(F_{y,f}(\alpha_f)\cos\delta + F_{y,r}(\alpha_r)\big)/m \end{bmatrix} + \mathbf{v}, \qquad \mathbf{v}\sim\mathcal{N}(0, R)$$

3. Two Ways to Push a Gaussian Through a Tyre

A Kalman filter keeps a Gaussian belief over the state, a mean $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ and a covariance $P$, and alternates between predicting it with the model and correcting it with the measurement. For linear models this is exact. Ours are not linear, and the two filters differ only in how they cope with that.

// Extended Kalman Filter: linearise the model at the estimate
$$\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathbf{x}}^-_k &= f(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1}, \mathbf{u}_{k-1}), & P^-_k &= F_k P_{k-1} F_k^\top + Q, & F_k &= \left.\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\right|_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-1}}\\ K_k &= P^-_k H_k^\top \big(H_k P^-_k H_k^\top + R\big)^{-1}, & \hat{\mathbf{x}}_k &= \hat{\mathbf{x}}^-_k + K_k\big(\mathbf{y}_k - h(\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-_k)\big), & H_k &= \left.\tfrac{\partial h}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\right|_{\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-_k} \end{aligned}$$
// Unscented Kalman Filter: push sample points through the model
$$\mathcal{X}_0 = \hat{\mathbf{x}}, \qquad \mathcal{X}_{\pm i} = \hat{\mathbf{x}} \pm \Big(\sqrt{(n+\lambda)\,P}\Big)_i, \qquad W_0 = \frac{\lambda}{n+\lambda}, \quad W_{\pm i} = \frac{1}{2(n+\lambda)}$$ $$\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \sum_i W_i\, h(\mathcal{X}_i), \qquad P_{yy} = \sum_i W_i\, (\mathcal{Y}_i - \hat{\mathbf{y}})(\cdot)^\top + R, \qquad K = P_{xy}\, P_{yy}^{-1}$$

With two states and the usual choice $n + \lambda = 3$, the UKF evaluates the model at five points: the mean, and $\pm\sqrt{3}$ standard deviations along each axis of the covariance.

EKF
Linearise the model, at one point
  • Uses the slope of the tyre curve exactly where the estimate is.
  • Needs Jacobians (analytic or by finite differences).
  • Exact when the uncertainty is small compared with the curvature.
UKF
Linearise the statistics, over the spread
  • Fits a line through points spread over the current uncertainty.
  • Derivative-free: only evaluates $f$ and $h$.
  • Captures curvature, but the points can land where the model no longer means much.

4. The Test Bench

Both filters run against a simulated car. The test bench has three parts: a "true" vehicle produces the motion, a sensor model turns it into what an ESC cluster would read, and both filters receive exactly the same samples.

A fair test bench has to be unfair to the filters in the right ways. A filter that knows the truth model exactly always looks brilliant, so the test bench builds in the mismatches a real car would have:

  • Imperfect tyre data. The filters' tyre stiffness is off by −6 % (front) and +4 % (rear), as if identified on a skid pad on a different day.
  • Missing dynamics. The true tyres build up force with a relaxation length of 0.6 m. The filters' tyres react instantly.
  • Unknown sensor offsets. Each run draws a new gyro and accelerometer offset that the filters do not estimate. The accelerometer channel in $R$ is inflated to cover the model error.

Four manoeuvres cover the range from boring to scary. Each one runs 30 times with fresh noise and offsets:

  1. Slalom: 72 km/h, ±30° at the steering wheel. Everyday driving, with the tyres in their linear range.
  2. Sine with dwell: the 0.7 Hz profile from the US ESC regulation (FMVSS 126) at 80 km/h and 70°. The rear axle saturates and the car comes close to spinning, $|\beta|$ peaks at 7°.
  3. Ramp steer: 80 km/h, steering wound up at 9°/s, so the lateral acceleration sweeps from zero all the way to the friction limit.
  4. Wet road: a slalom on $\mu = 0.5$, while both filters still believe they are on dry asphalt.

5. Results

Everyday driving and the limit: a tie

Look at the slalom and the two near-limit manoeuvres, and the honest summary is that the two filters produce the same answer. The traces lie on top of each other. Over 30 runs the RMSE differs by at most 0.015°, far below what any reference sensor could resolve. Even where the lateral acceleration exceeds 6 m/s², the UKF's edge in the ramp steer is 0.02°.

Two more observations from the table:

  • Cost is not a differentiator either. Both take about 0.2 ms per step without any optimisation, a fiftieth of the 10 ms sample period. With finite-difference Jacobians the EKF evaluates the model five times per step, exactly as many as the UKF's five sigma points. Analytic Jacobians would make the EKF cheaper, at the price of a page of derivatives that must be re-derived whenever the tyre model changes.
  • The wet road breaks both, equally. Neither filter knows that $\mu$ has halved, so both trust a tyre that does not exist. Their RMSE of 0.6° is almost a quarter of a signal that peaks at 2.6°, and, worse, the true $\beta$ lies inside their ±2σ band only 19 % of the time. They are wrong and confident about it.

Restarting at the limit: where the UKF earns its keep

The tie above has a reason. Started from rest, both filters track the car from the first sample, so their uncertainty stays small, and a small Gaussian sees a nearly straight piece of tyre curve. The interesting case is when the uncertainty is large at the worst possible moment: say the estimator is (re)initialised while the car is already sliding at the limit, with $\hat\beta = 0$ and an honest initial uncertainty.

In the ramp steer, the restart happens at $a_y = 9.8$ m/s² with the true $\beta$ at −6.7°. Within 40 ms the EKF has moved to −2.6°, a good first step. But in the same update its standard deviation collapses from 5° to 0.17°. From then on it trusts its wrong estimate far more than the measurements and creeps towards the truth over seconds. The UKF lands at −5.5° with σ ≈ 1.3° after the same 40 ms, and is within half a degree of the truth half a second later.

Figure 3 explains why. At $\hat\beta = 0$ the EKF believes the tyres are in their linear range, where the tangent is steep, and concludes that the accelerometer pins $\beta$ down precisely. The UKF's sigma points sit $\pm\sqrt{3}\cdot 5° \approx \pm 8.7°$ apart and reach into the saturated part of the curve. The UKF sees the force flatten, keeps a realistic covariance, and keeps listening to the sensors. (Try the restart at the limit preset in Figure 3.)

The price of a wide spread

The same mechanism shows up with process noise. A larger $Q$ is the usual way to make a filter react faster and to make up for model error, and it also widens the sigma points at every step. In the ramp steer:

The reason is an old friend from statistics, Jensen's inequality. Near the peak the tyre curve is concave, so the average force over a wide spread of slip angles is lower than the force at the average slip angle. The UKF computes that average faithfully and predicts less lateral acceleration than the car is producing. To explain the measurement it pushes $\hat\beta$ further out, overshooting by up to about 2° in these runs. The EKF never averages, so it never makes this error, and it never sees the flattening either.

6. Lessons Learned

  1. The model is the bottleneck, not the filter. Under normal conditions the choice between EKF and UKF moves the error by hundredths of a degree. A wrong friction coefficient moves it by more than half a degree, and no filter variant fixes that.
  2. Choose the filter for its failure mode. The UKF is the better choice when the uncertainty can be large at the limit: initialisation, a sensor dropout, the transition into a skid. The EKF is simpler to reason about and degrades gracefully when it is over-tuned.
  3. The UKF's spread is a design parameter. $P_0$, $Q$ and the scaling parameters decide where the sigma points land. If they land in physically meaningless states, the filter averages over nonsense. Clipping sigma points to plausible ranges is cheap insurance.
  4. Check consistency, not just RMSE. "Is the truth inside ±2σ as often as it should be?" flagged the wet road (19 %) and the collapsed EKF (10 %) immediately. An RMSE alone would only have said "a bit worse".
  5. Estimate what you do not know. The natural next step is to add $\mu$ (or a tyre-stiffness scale) to the state, use GNSS velocity whenever it is available, or learn the tyre's residual from data. All of these attack the real source of error found here.

Conclusion

Estimating the side-slip angle is a small problem with all the ingredients of state estimation: an unmeasurable quantity, cheap and biased sensors, and a model that is accurate right up to the point where it matters most. Putting the two filters side by side showed that the textbook ranking, "UKF beats EKF on nonlinear problems", is true only where the uncertainty is large compared with the curvature of the model. That happens rarely, but exactly at the friction limit, where an ESC system needs the estimate most. There the UKF's sigma points are both its strength and its weakness, which makes tuning their spread as important as choosing the filter.

Reproduce it

All figures and tables above can be reproduced with a single NumPy script of about 500 lines: truth model, sensor model, both filters and all three experiments. It needs nothing but Python and NumPy and takes about three minutes for 30 runs per case.
